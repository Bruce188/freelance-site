Case · Customs intake · Prototype

From invoice and packing list to one email with every open question.

I built a tool for customs declarants. On a fictitious import shipment from China it read the invoice and packing list, suggested CN codes for five lines, found nine open items (including a carton count that differs between the two documents) and wrote one email with eight questions for the client.

Input
Commercial invoice (PDF) and packing list
Output
CN code suggestions, checklist, email to the client
Status
Working prototype, tested on fictitious shipments

The problem

I spent three years in UPS's customs brokerage team in Diegem. What I saw every day: a declarant opens a new shipment, notices the EORI number is missing, emails the client, gets an answer, only then sees that freight is not on the FOB invoice, and emails again. Every round costs a day. On top of that, every goods line needs a CN code, and a vague description means searching.

The goal: find everything that is missing in one pass, and email the client once.

Approach

  1. Read the documentsInvoice and packing list, as PDF or text. Scans too.
  2. Extract the dataParties, Incoterm, values, weights, origin per line, and where the documents contradict each other.
  3. Suggest CN codesUp to three codes per line, with the reasoning and a link to TARIC.
  4. Checklist and emailFixed rules for what an import declaration needs, and one draft email with all the questions.

Three deliberate choices:

  • Suggestions, not decisions. The tool files nothing. The declarant checks every code in TARIC.
  • Ask instead of guess. When the code depends on a missing fact (porcelain or stoneware?), that question goes in the email.
  • The checklist is plain code. What is required lives in simple rules you can change without touching the AI.

What the tool produced

Below is real output from the tool on the test shipment: five goods lines, FOB Shanghai, with errors put in on purpose. All companies and numbers are fictitious.

Result on the test shipment

3 of 5 linesgot a high-confidence code. For the knives it matched the code on the invoice.
9 open itemsfound, including the 24 versus 26 cartons between invoice and packing list.
1 risk flaggedPossible anti-dumping duties on ceramics from China, on exactly the line where origin was missing.
1 email, 8 questionsin one go, instead of a new email for each missing piece.

A second test shipment (a nearly complete UK invoice, DAP Antwerp) gave one open question: is the throw a blanket or a bedspread? That decides the code.

What is still to do

To be clear: this is a prototype. So far it has only seen fictitious shipments. The next step is testing on real, anonymised files together with a declarant, to measure how often the codes are right and how much time it saves per file. Emails in Dutch and French are on the list too.

The CN codes are suggestions to verify in TARIC. The tool does not file declarations and does not replace the declarant.
